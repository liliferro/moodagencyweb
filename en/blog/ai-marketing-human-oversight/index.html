AI in marketing: a useful pilot with human oversight

How to choose a task, protect information and evaluate results before expanding your use of AI.

By Mood Agency · November 13, 2026

The first question isn't which model to buy, but which task needs improvement. Drafting, classifying information and summarizing documents require different criteria. A useful pilot defines the work, acceptable errors and who approves the output before it's used.

Choose a task with clear boundaries

Start with a frequent activity whose output you can review—for example, turning an approved brief into an initial content outline. Define what information may be used, what must remain exact and which decisions stay with the team.

Don't evaluate AI only with an easy prompt. Collect everyday examples, ambiguous cases and incomplete inputs. A tool may produce good writing but struggle when a brief is contradictory or lacks enough information.

Build a review rubric

Assess accuracy, tone, usefulness and adherence to the brief separately. A draft can sound good and contain a fabricated number. Another may be correct but unsuitable for the channel or audience. Scoring these dimensions separately helps identify what needs to change.

A simple rubric asks whether every factual claim has support, names and conditions are preserved, the text meets its objective and it introduces promises the brand can't substantiate. Reviewers must be able to reject output without pressure to use it.

Protect information from the start

Define which materials may be shared with each tool. Replace personal data with examples when it isn't needed. Check permissions, provider terms and internal policies before connecting client repositories. Access to a folder doesn't mean all of its contents belong in a pilot.

Document risks and ownership

NIST offers a voluntary framework for managing AI risks during design, use and evaluation. Its generative AI profile, published in 2024, addresses risks specific to this technology. In practice, document your pilot's purpose, boundaries, controls and the person responsible for reviewing them.

Measure the whole workflow

Compare total time: preparation, generation, review and correction. Include the number and severity of errors, not just the speed of the first response. If reviewing an output means rebuilding it, the apparent productivity gain can disappear.

Before expanding the pilot, decide which results justify continuing and which failures require a pause. Keep approved and rejected examples to train the team. Oversight works best when it's designed into the process rather than improvised at the end.
